Segregation, income polarization, and spatial autocorrelation belong to separate
literatures, though each asks how an attribute is arranged across a map. Their
measures fit in one table, set by two choices: whether the attribute keeps the
size of each unit's departure from the regional composition, and whether the
graph of neighboring units is read by its sum or by an eigenvalue. One
combination is unused. The project fills it with a spatial concordance index,
*K*, which joins neighboring units that depart from the regional composition in
the same direction and reports the share of the achievable concordance a map
attains, net of chance.

*K* splits exactly into the two sides of an income threshold, so sweeping the
threshold locates where in a region's income distribution its geography is most
organized, and how that organization divides between the region's more and less
affluent neighborhoods. California's metropolitan areas are the first
application. The empirical sequel, *Separated, Polarized, and Factioned?*,
covers every U.S. metropolitan region from 2010 to 2025.
